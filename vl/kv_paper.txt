\subsection{Multimodal Large Language Models}
  The remarkable reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs)~\cite{gpt4,deepseek} have motivated extending them beyond text-only scenarios
  to visual understanding, giving rise to multimodal large language models (MLLMs)~\cite{llava1.5,llavanext,qwen3vl}. A typical MLLM consists of
  three main components: a vision encoder, a multimodal projector, and an LLM. The vision encoder converts an input image into visual features, and
  the projector maps these features into the LLM's input space. The resulting visual tokens are then fed into the LLM together with textual tokens
  for multimodal understanding and generation.
  However, the number of visual tokens grows rapidly with image resolution and video length, and these tokens are often highly redundant. For
  example, LLaVA-NeXT~\cite{llavanext} adopts the AnyRes technique to support higher-resolution inputs, producing 2,880 visual tokens for a single
  672 $\times$ 672 image. In the video domain, LongVA~\cite{longva} can process up to 2,000 frames, yielding over 200K visual tokens. Such a large
  number of visual tokens leads to a substantial KV cache, posing significant challenges to memory consumption and generation efficiency.
  Consequently, visual token pruning, which aims to enable efficient MLLM inference by removing unimportant visual tokens, has become an active
  research direction.

  \subsection{Visual Token Pruning}
  Visual token pruning methods estimate token importance and retain the most relevant visual tokens. The first category, similarity-based methods,
  removes redundant tokens based on feature similarity. DivPrune~\cite{divprune} greedily selects visual tokens by maximizing the minimum cosine
  distance between them, favoring a diverse and less redundant subset. DART~\cite{dart} selects a small set of pivot tokens and prunes the rest based
  on their similarity to the pivots, while HoloV~\cite{holov} adaptively allocates the token budget across image crops according to their
  information distribution, avoiding over-pruning in information-rich regions. The second category, attention-based methods, instead relies on
  attention scores. FastV~\cite{fastv} identifies important tokens using attention scores from the early layers of the LLM, while

  on their similarity to the pivots, while HoloV~\cite{holov} adaptively allocates the token budget across image crops according to their
  information distribution, avoiding over-pruning in information-rich regions. The second category, attention-based methods, instead relies on
  attention scores. FastV~\cite{fastv} identifies important tokens using attention scores from the early layers of the LLM, while
  SparseVLM~\cite{sparsevlm} progressively prunes less important tokens across decoder layers. Building on FastV, HAWK~\cite{hawk} assigns different
  weights to attention heads when aggregating the scores. The third category combines the above two signals. VisionZip~\cite{visionzip} selects
  dominant tokens using attention scores within the vision encoder and merges the remaining ones into complementary tokens, while
  CDPruner~\cite{cdpruner} jointly considers instruction relevance and inter-token redundancy to prune and merge visual tokens. However, these
  methods all rely on hand-crafted heuristic rules, which limits their robustness across tasks and their performance. In this paper, we address these
  limitations with a learnable approach.
